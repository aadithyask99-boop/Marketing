A few days ago we wrote [our take on the Claude Motion launch](/blogs/claude-motion-ai-motion-design/). Since then the same question keeps landing in our inbox. Has anyone actually used it, and is it any good?

It is a fair question, and it is harder to answer than it looks. So we went looking for people who have really made something with it.

**We have not run Claude Motion ourselves yet.** It is a beta on Team and Enterprise plans, and we would rather tell you that now than imply an experience we have not had. What follows is what other people report, with each source linked, plus the tests we plan to run when we get our hands on it.

## Who Has Actually Used It?

Not many people, and that matters. Within days of launch the web filled with explainers of Claude Motion, and most of them are rewrites of the announcement. Some are upfront about it. One review says plainly that none of its points have been verified by independent testing yet.

The honest summary is that we have one real hands-on account and a lot of echo. That is normal in the first week of a beta, and it is exactly why we are cautious about big claims in either direction.

## What Early Users Report Works

Here is what holds up across the sources.

**The first draft arrives without a blank timeline.** Anthropic's own examples are turning a quarterly report into a 30-second all-hands explainer, adding motion to a chart in a board deck and building a product walkthrough for customer onboarding. You type `/motion`, describe what you want and Claude builds it.

**Edits are real edits.** Because Claude Motion [writes code rather than generating video](https://claude.com/resources/articles/dashboards-and-motion), you can change a word, a number or a timing without regenerating the whole clip. The eesel.ai review calls this the best feature, because changing one thing does not disturb the rest.

**A good brief changes the result.** Charlie Hills made all 16 of his videos from the same long pasted brief plus a one-line description of each scene. He describes the output as fine by default and closer to a product launch with the brief behind it. That is a single creator's judgement, but it fits something we see with every AI tool. The brief does most of the work.

<blockquote><p>"The change is the point."</p><footer>A line from the brief Charlie Hills used for his 16 Claude Motion videos</footer></blockquote>

**It is built for your own material.** Anthropic describes Claude Motion as made for explaining your own work, and it says outright that there is no generated footage and no AI-generated people. Reviewers read that as a strength for data explainers and branded graphics, and a limit for anything that needs footage or presenters.

## Where It Struggles

The limits are just as consistent, and a few of them are practical.

**Builds take longer than the tool says.** Hills reports that Claude Motion estimates one to four minutes per video. His 16 took about 12 to 15 minutes each, and only three could render at once, with a fourth request waiting its turn. Treat that as one person's report, but it is a useful reality check if you are planning a deadline around it.

**The default look can be generic.** A short prompt with no direction tends to produce what every other short prompt produces. This is true of most AI tools, and it is the main reason the brief matters.

**Access is narrow.** Motion is in beta on Team and Enterprise at launch. Enterprise admins have to switch it on under Organization settings. If you pay for a personal plan, you may not be able to try it at all yet.

**The cost is unclear.** The eesel.ai review says there is no separate fee and no published price per animation, so a longer or more complex animation uses more of your plan allowance. Until someone measures it, budget for a bit of trial and error.

None of this is a reason to dismiss it. A first draft in a quarter of an hour, with every word editable, is still a big change from building an explainer by hand. It is a reason to plan with real numbers instead of the launch estimate.

## Writing the Brief That Does the Work

Everything above points the same way. The brief is the product.

Claude Motion needs the same things any good human brief needs, and the same habits apply as with any prompt. Say who it is for, say what you want them to remember and say what the tool must not do. If you want a refresher, our guides on [how to write a really good AI prompt](/blogs/how-to-write-a-really-good-ai-prompt/), [role prompting and prompt length](/blogs/role-prompting-and-prompt-length/) and [how to improve AI responses with follow-up prompts](/blogs/how-to-improve-ai-responses-with-follow-up-prompts/) all apply here.

Hills shares a structured brief with sections for the quality bar, format, a single rule, the look, the motion, what to avoid and a final check. We have adapted that idea into a template of our own, and we will use it for our tests.

The "Brand" line is the one that most people leave empty. That brings us to the part we care most about.

## Where Brand Decides the Result

Claude Motion can make an animation in minutes. It cannot decide what your brand looks like.

Anthropic's launch article does not describe a brand kit feature, so assume the tool can only follow rules that exist. If your colours, fonts, tone and logo rules live in someone's head, the animation will land somewhere near your brand and probably not on it. That is the quiet risk with every fast AI tool. It makes it cheaper to be off brand at scale.

This is where a proper brand identity earns its keep. Written palette values, a type system, a voice and clear logo rules are exactly the inputs that make a brief for Claude Motion work. They are also what keeps the output from sounding like everyone else's. If you do not have them yet, that is worth fixing before you animate anything. ## Who Should Try It Now, and Who Can Wait

**Try it now** if your workspace already has it and you regularly make short explainers, chart animations or onboarding walkthroughs. Use a small internal brief first, write your brand rules down and keep a person on any numbers or claims.

**Wait** if you are on a personal plan, if you need real footage or presenters, or if you were hoping the tool would come up with the idea for you. It will not, and early users say so.

One more thing for anyone thinking about search. A video is a good way to explain an idea, but search engines and AI answer engines can only use what they can read. Pair any explainer with clear text on the page around it.
